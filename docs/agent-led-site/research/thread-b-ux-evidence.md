# Thread B: UX evidence for an agent-led, generative-UI website

Scope: what the published evidence says about a website whose UI is generated per visitor by an AI agent and driven by chat. Covers chat versus navigation, generative and adaptive UI usability, latency and abandonment, accessibility, trust and AI disclosure, and the blank-prompt problem.

Method: Firecrawl search and scrape, run on 2026-10-01. I read every source below in full or in its relevant section. Numbers are quoted as the source states them. Strength scale: 5 = normative standard, law or large probability sample; 4 = peer-reviewed controlled study; 3 = large industry survey, vendor-run evaluation or qualitative expert study; 2 = expert opinion or small qualitative sample; 1 = anecdote.

## Design implications

1. **Keep the conventional site underneath and make chat optional.** Chat increases cognitive load and reduces autonomy compared with menus (F1). Users switch to conventional sources when stakes are high (F2). Visitors expect a user-initiated helper in a predictable place, not a site-initiated one (F17). The agent should add to crawlable, navigable pages, not replace them. Do not open the chat or the agent proactively.
2. **Never open on an empty prompt.** Use starter chips that show concrete capabilities, plus a GUI path. The articulation barrier (F3), new users' confusion and "Can you" probing (F5), and NN/g's prompt-control findings (F4) all point the same way. Chips must carry plain-language labels. Icon-only or branded labels failed in testing (F4). The evidence that starters help is qualitative. Nothing I found measures starters versus none in a controlled test, so A/B test it ourselves.
3. **Fix the frame and generate inside it.** Hold navigation, help entry and brand chrome in a fixed relative order on every page. Generate only the content region. WCAG 3.2.3 and 3.2.6 require this for repeated mechanisms (F15). Predictability loss is the main genUI risk (F8). Adaptive-UI research shows accuracy matters more than novelty (F9).
4. **Use a component catalog, not freeform page generation.** Freeform genUI takes "a minute or two", and streaming roughly halves that (F7). Expert pages still beat it head-to-head (F6). The page-speed literature ties every 0.1 s to funnel progression (F12). Compose pre-built, accessible components from agent output, and serve a static first paint with no model in the critical path.
5. **Budget latency by perception thresholds, not tokens per second.** Acknowledge within 0.1 s (F11). Show visible and announced progress before 1 s. Stream content well before 10 s (F11). F13 shows a 2-20 s TTFT did not change behaviour in a captive study and that faster replies were rated less thoughtful. So do not artificially rush trivial "thinking" UI. But F13 does not measure abandonment on an open website. Treat F12 and F11 as the binding constraints for first paint and first feedback.
6. **Make the stream accessible by design.** Announce "thinking…", "done" and errors with a polite status region (WCAG 4.1.3, F14), not the whole token stream. W3C warns against being "too chatty" (F14). Return focus predictably after each reply. Audit with a real screen reader. Over 80% of deployed chatbots fail, and automated tools under-detect the problems (F16).
7. **Disclose the AI once, early and plainly, and lead with the benefit.** Disclosure is legally required in the EU unless it is obvious (F25). Being exposed later is worse than self-disclosing (F18). But disclosure and the word "AI" lower trust and purchase intent (F18, F19, F20). So disclose at first interaction in a factual, non-buzzword way and avoid "AI-powered" as a headline claim. Heavy AI users react positively (F19), and about half of US adults now use chatbots (F21), so this cost may shrink for the target audience.
8. **Ground every factual claim, especially pricing, policy and capability.** Hallucinations cause outsized negative word of mouth (F24). Most users don't retry after one bad experience (F23). Answer from site content only, cite the page, and say "I don't know" with a link to the canonical page or a human. Following HAX G1 and G2, state up front what the concierge can and cannot do (F10). Openness about limits mitigates hallucination damage (F24).
9. **Always offer a visible exit to a human or a conventional page.** 87% say human access is essential (F23). 64% would prefer no AI in service and 53% would consider switching (F22). HAX G8 and G9 call for efficient dismissal and correction (F10).

## Strongest counter-evidence

Evidence that works against an agent-led, generative-UI site:

- **Chat is not better than menus for most structured tasks.** In the one controlled comparison found (F1), the chatbot lost on autonomy, cognitive load and satisfaction. The broadest current behaviour study (F2) shows users abandon AI when accuracy matters. A B2B buyer evaluating a product is exactly that high-stakes case. A chat-first homepage could reduce performance relative to a well-structured conventional site.
- **The best genUI evidence comes from the vendor and excludes speed.** Google's 82.8% preference (F6) used pre-cached results and excluded generation time, which the same paper puts at "a minute or two" (F7). Even then, human-expert pages won. No independent study I found shows per-visitor generated UI beating a designed page on task success or conversion.
- **AI disclosure has a measured trust cost, and the law requires disclosure.** Disclosure lowered trust in 13 of 13 preregistered experiments (F18) and in newsroom field data (F19). The word "AI" lowered purchase intent (F20). Concealment is not an option: exposure is worse (F18), and EU Article 50 mandates disclosure (F25). An agent-led site therefore carries a built-in trust cost.
- **Unforgiving failure economics.** Only 27% retry a chatbot after a bad experience (F23). Hallucinations amplify negative word of mouth (F24). A small error rate on a first-visit marketing site may cost more than the personalization gains.
- **Latency evidence cuts both ways, and the favourable half is weak.** The CHI '26 study (F13) suggests slow answers are tolerated and even read as thoughtful. But participants were paid and could not leave. The page-speed studies (F12) measure real visitors who can leave and point the other way. Both are indirect for streaming chat. No study I found measures abandonment as a function of TTFT on a public website, so this remains unverified.
- **Accessibility debt is the default outcome.** Over 80% of deployed chatbots have a critical accessibility issue (F16). WCAG consistency rules (F15) constrain how much of the layout can change per visitor.

## Gaps (not found or not verified)

- No controlled study comparing starter chips with an empty prompt box on task success or engagement. Evidence for chips is qualitative (F4, F5).
- No public data on abandonment versus time-to-first-token for website chat.
- Google PAIR Guidebook was not read in this pass. Microsoft HAX (F10) covers the same guideline space.
- Baymard has no LLM-concierge study among the sources read here. Its live-chat finding (F17) predates LLMs.
