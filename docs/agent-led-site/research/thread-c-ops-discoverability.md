Source types: **primary-law** (statute or regulation text), **primary-vendor** (first-party docs or price list), **peer/preprint** (academic paper), **panel/log study** (measured data published by a research org or vendor), **secondary** (law-firm or press summary of a primary source), **anecdote**.

## Implications for the KnowMe site

1. **Do not put content only behind the chat.** AI crawlers read raw HTML and run no JavaScript (F1). Google renders JS, but slowly at the tail (F2) and still recommends server or pre-rendering (F3). Chat answers are generated per request and have no URL, so no crawler can index them under any rendering strategy. The KnowMe client is a Vite SPA. Every claim we want found needs a pre-rendered, linkable page; the agent should be a layer over those pages, not a replacement for them.
2. **Earned citations beat on-site tricks.** GEO edits can help (F4), but AI engines favour third-party sources over brand-owned pages (F5), and llms.txt has shown no effect (F6). Ship llms.txt only as a cheap extra. Spend effort on crawlable pages and third-party coverage.
3. **Search referral traffic is shrinking for informational queries.** Expect fewer clicks where AI summaries appear (F7, F8). Being cited in the summary matters most (F9). Google disputes the decline (F10). Plan the funnel so direct, branded and referral visitors are the main audience for the agent, and do not count on informational-query SEO traffic.
4. **Blocking: the current model contract probably cannot serve a public site.** `k8s/base/uar-configmap.yaml` and `docker-compose.yaml` point UAR at `qwen3.8-max` through the "Qwen Token Plan". Both Token Plan editions forbid "custom application backends" and threaten suspension or key bans (F13), and the quota pauses service when spent. A public website agent is an application backend. Move to pay-as-you-go Model Studio (F11) or get written confirmation from Alibaba before launch. Token Plan also routes prompts to the Singapore "Global" deployment with cross-border transfer, which feeds the GDPR record (F28-F30).
5. **Unit cost is small per session; the risk is the tail.** At $2 in / $6 out per 1M tokens (F11), a session of 30,000 input and 2,400 output tokens costs about $0.06 + $0.014 = **$0.075** (our arithmetic, not a published figure). Implicit caching of a stable system prompt above 1,024 tokens bills cached input at 20% (F12). That is well under Intercom's $0.99 per resolution (F14), but session length is extremely skewed (F15), so cap per-session tokens and turns and log real distributions in the first weeks.
6. **Spend caps belong in the host layer, not the prompt.** Denial of wallet is a named risk (F16). Daily cost is bounded only by the provider quota (F18), and most traffic is bots, with APIs a main target (F22). Enforce per-IP and per-session token budgets, max input size, timeouts and a global daily spend ceiling with a static-page fallback in nginx/UAR. Bot challenges such as Turnstile or proof-of-work are speed bumps with weak efficacy evidence (F23). Use them on the chat endpoint only, never in front of content pages.
7. **Assume the agent will be jailbroken, and make what it says safe to be wrong about.** Prompt injection cannot be fully prevented (F17). Viral attention brings thousands of attempts (F19). The operator is liable for chatbot statements (F20), and bad output is a brand event (F21). The agent must not quote prices, commit to terms, or claim capabilities without pointing to a canonical page. Claims about KnowMe's AI must be substantiated (F27).
8. **Disclose upfront, once, in the first message.** Art. 50 applies now: clear AI disclosure at first interaction, accessible (F24). Upfront clear and conspicuous disclosure also meets California §17941 (F25) and Utah's safe harbor (F26). One design satisfies all three.
9. **Treat transcripts and personalisation as personal data.** Keep personalisation state in the browser where possible; once it is sent to the server it is in ePrivacy and GDPR scope (F29). Set a short default retention for chat logs, warn visitors not to share sensitive data, and publish the legal basis and processor chain, including Alibaba Cloud (F28, F30).

## Strongest counter-evidence

- **Google renders JavaScript well** (F2: 100% of 100k+ fetches rendered, median delay 10 s). For Google Search alone, a well-built SPA is indexable, so the "AI site is invisible" risk is mainly about non-Google AI engines (F1) and about chat-only content, not about React itself.
- **The click-loss evidence is contested and moving.** Google says total organic clicks are "relatively stable" (F10). Seer measured an 85% rebound in AIO-query CTR from Dec 2025 to Feb 2026 (F9). Ahrefs' 58% is correlational (F8).
- **The F1 crawler finding is nearly two years old** and did not test Claude-SearchBot, Claude-User or newer OpenAI fetchers. Vendors could have added rendering since. Re-test with our own server logs.
- **Real abuse losses at public marketing chatbots are mostly reputational and small in money.** Air Canada paid about CAD 650 (F20). Fullpath reports 99% of 3,000 manipulation attempts failed (F19). The $46,000/day figure is a theoretical ceiling for stolen cloud keys, not a website chatbot bill (F18).
- **Regulatory exposure may be low for a disclosed, non-sales-pressure agent.** California §17941 needs intent to mislead (F25). Utah's general duty is triggered only when asked (F26). A €15M GDPR fine on a chatbot maker was overturned in court (F28).
- **Per-session cost is cheap** (implication 5). Cost overrun requires abuse or a missing cap, not normal use.
